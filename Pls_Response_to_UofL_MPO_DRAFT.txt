         PLAINTIFFS' RESPONSE TO UofL DEFENDANTS' MOTION FOR
                           PROTECTIVE ORDER

       Plaintiffs, by counsel, respectfully submit this Response in opposition to the

Motion for Protective Order filed by Defendants UofL Health-Louisville, Inc.,

University of Louisville Physicians, Inc., and UofL Health, Inc. (collectively, "UofL

Defendants"). For the reasons set forth below, the UofL Defendants' Motion should

be denied in its entirety.

                                   INTRODUCTION

       Thelma "Timmi" Tinker underwent a PET scan on August 15, 2022. The results

showed findings indicative of potential pulmonary malignancy. Almost two years

later—in May 2024—Timmi learned for the first time that her PET scan had ever

reflected anything alarming. She is now dead.

       At the center of this case is a simple but devastating question: what happened

to that PET scan result? Who, if anyone, actually reviewed it? Was it ever

communicated to Timmi? Did anyone acknowledge it, and if so, how? Did the

"acknowledgment" in UofL's Epic EHR system require an actual human being to open

and read the result—or could it have been triggered automatically or by accident?

Was Timmi's electronic record accessed and examined—perhaps by someone

attempting to understand what had happened before the family began asking

questions? What does the produced audit data actually mean, and what critical fields

are missing from what UofL was compelled by court order to produce?

       These questions are not abstract. They can only be answered by someone with

knowledge of how UofL's specific EHR systems work—their configuration, their audit

trails, their notification and acknowledgment logic—in the context of Timmi's actual

records. That is precisely what Plaintiffs' CR 30.02(6) Notice seeks.

       Rather than participating in this discovery, UofL has moved for a protective

order. It ignores months of stonewalling that required Plaintiffs to move to compel

and obtain a court order. It ignores that UofL scrambled to produce records at 3:50

p.m. on the deadline day—10 minutes before the court-ordered 4:00 p.m. cutoff—and

that what UofL produced omits the very audit data fields that would answer the key

questions in this case. And it ignores that the audit data UofL has produced contains

entries that raise serious questions requiring live system explanation.

       UofL's motion is an attempt to evade thorough and proper discovery in a case

involving a woman's preventable death. The Motion should be denied.

                               FACTUAL BACKGROUND

       On April 13, 2026, Plaintiffs served detailed interrogatories and requests for

production focused squarely on the August 15, 2022 PET CT scan result: who

accessed it in the EMR, how it was routed through Epic's In-Basket system, how the

"acknowledged" function works, what the MyChart audit trail shows, and whether

there is any record of the result ever being communicated to Timmi. UofL's responses

were due May 13, 2026. UofL did not respond.

       After multiple unsuccessful follow-up communications, Plaintiffs were forced

to file a motion to compel on May 21, 2026. The Court granted that motion on May

26, 2026, ordering UofL to "fully and completely respond to this discovery by 4:00

p.m. on Friday, May 29, 2026."

       At 3:50 p.m. on May 29—ten minutes before the court-ordered deadline—UofL

produced its response. The production included seven documents: internal Epic

workflow guides and tip sheets, and Timmi's MyChart messages. What UofL did not

produce: the EMR audit trails showing who accessed the PET scan result and when,

the MyChart audit log showing patient-side interactions with the scan result, the

In-Basket data, the PACS migration data, the IP address data, the patient

notification records, the record-specific access logs, and the print-related details—

the very records that are the subject of Plaintiffs' interrogatories and the

cornerstone of any meaningful forensic understanding of what happened to Timmi's

scan result.

       Two days after that deficient production, and with those critical gaps

unaddressed, UofL filed the present Motion for Protective Order seeking to prevent

Plaintiffs from exploring those exact gaps through a duly noticed CR 30.02(6)

deposition.

                                      ARGUMENT

       CR 26.03 authorizes a protective order only where "justice requires to protect

a party from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense."

The party seeking protection bears the burden of showing good cause. Morrow v.

Brown, Todd & Heyburn, 957 S.W.2d 722, 724 (Ky. App. 1997). UofL has not met

and cannot meet that burden.

I.     PLAINTIFFS' CR 30.02(6) NOTICE DESCRIBES THE TOPICS WITH
       REASONABLE PARTICULARITY.

       CR 30.02(6) requires that a corporate representative notice "describe with

reasonable particularity the matters on which examination is requested." UofL argues

that the Notice fails this standard. The argument is wrong.

       Plaintiffs' Notice identifies eighteen topics, each of which is directed to one

or more specific systems (PACS, MyChart/Epic, In-Basket), one or more specific

events or time periods (the August 15, 2022 PET scan and its routing), one or more

specific functions (acknowledgment, release to patient, print), and specific persons

(Dr. Gettis, Dr. Aria, Timmi). Every topic traces back to the same central inquiry:

how did UofL's EHR systems handle this particular scan result for this particular

patient, and what does the audit data tell us about what happened? That is not a

"boundless" inquiry. It is a focused, technically complex one.

       "Reasonable particularity" does not mean surgical precision. It means

sufficient description to enable the corporation to identify and prepare the

appropriate witness(es). See, e.g., Reed v. Bennett, 312 F.3d 1190, 1196 (10th Cir.

2002) (topics must be adequate to "apprise the corporation of the areas in which the

witness is expected to testify"). Plaintiffs' Notice more than satisfies that standard:

each topic is described in detail, supported by defined terms in Appendix A, and

accompanied by explanatory footnotes identifying relevant systems (e.g., footnotes 3

and 4 explaining PACS).

       UofL's objection to the "catchall" provision—the statement that "the

deposition may cover any topics within the general knowledge of the designee(s), not

limited to the above-specific items"—is misplaced. That language does not transform

the deposition into an unlimited inquiry. It is standard advisory language alerting

the designee to be prepared. UofL's counsel may object at the deposition to any

question that strays beyond the noticed topics. The proper mechanism for policing

scope is an objection at the deposition, not a pre-emptive protective order that blocks

the deposition entirely or narrows all eighteen topics before UofL has even identified

its designees.

II.    THE NOTICED TOPICS APPROPRIATELY SEEK UofL'S CORPORATE
       FACTUAL KNOWLEDGE AND INSTITUTIONAL POSITIONS.

       UofL objects that Topics 2, 3, 4, 11, and 14 "improperly seek legal conclusions

and privileged information." This characterization misstates both what the topics

ask and what CR 30.02(6) permits.

       A.     Topics 2, 3, and 4 seek UofL's corporate positions on its own
              employees' responsibilities and on any comparative fault defense
              it intends to assert.

       Topic 2 asks whether UofL admits that Dr. Gettis—its own employee and

agent—had a responsibility to communicate the PET scan result to Timmi. UofL is

not merely a passive observer of this litigation. It has answered the complaint and

presumably has taken, or will take, institutional positions about whether its

physician had an obligation to communicate critical diagnostic results. Topic 2 asks

what that position is. That is exactly the kind of information a corporate

representative deposition is designed to elicit. A corporation must be able to testify

about the knowledge "reasonably available to the organization." CR 30.02(6).

       Topics 3 and 4 ask whether UofL contends that Timmi had an "established

standard of care" or "duty" with respect to using MyChart, being aware of test

results, and understanding clinical findings. These are not abstract legal theory

questions posed to the corporation as legal expert. They directly reflect defenses UofL

may assert: comparative fault, contributory negligence, or assumption of risk. If UofL

intends to argue to the jury that Timmi Tinker bore some responsibility for her own

delayed diagnosis because she had access to MyChart and could have seen her own

scan results, then UofL's corporate position on those topics is exactly what Plaintiffs

need to explore and what the corporate designee must be prepared to address.

       B.     Topics 11 and 14 go to core disputed facts about which UofL
              must have, and commit to, a corporate position.

       Topic 11 asks whether UofL contends that Dr. Gettis actually read the August

15, 2022 PET scan report, and if so, when and with what support. Topic 14 asks

whether UofL contends that Dr. Aria had a responsibility to communicate the results

further than he did. These are among the most critical factual questions in this case.

       Courts have routinely recognized that 30(b)(6)-equivalent depositions may

include "contention topics" that ask the corporation to commit to its factual

positions. See, e.g., Brazos River Auth. v. GE Ionics, Inc., 469 F.3d 416, 432–34 (5th

Cir. 2006) (contention topics are permissible; 30(b)(6) designees must be prepared to

testify about the company's position). Asking whether UofL "contends" that Dr.

Gettis read the report is not asking for a legal conclusion—it is asking whether UofL,

as an institution, has any evidentiary basis for the position that its physician

reviewed this patient's critical scan result. If UofL has no document and no witness

who can testify to that fact, then it should say so. If it does, it must identify the

document and commit to the position.

       None of these topics invades attorney-client privilege. They ask about

institutional facts and corporate positions—not about what lawyers told clients or

what litigation strategy has been devised. To the extent any specific question at the

deposition intrudes on privilege, UofL's counsel may assert that objection then.

III.   UofL'S PROCEDURAL CHALLENGE TO THE CR 34 INSPECTION
       REQUEST IGNORES THE PLAIN TEXT OF CR 30.02(5) AND THE
       REALITIES OF THIS CASE.

       A.     CR 30.02(5) expressly permits accompanying inspection requests.

       UofL argues that Plaintiffs violated CR 34.02's 30-day response period by

including an inspection request in a deposition notice. This argument conflates two

different mechanisms.

       CR 30.02(5) states plainly that a deposition notice "may be accompanied by a

request made in compliance with Rule 34 for the production of documents and

tangible things at the taking of the deposition. The procedure of Rule 34.02 shall

apply to the request." CR 30.02(5) (emphasis added). The plain meaning of this

provision is that the inspection or production occurs "at the taking of the deposition"—

not 30 days later. The 30-day period in CR 34.02(2) governs written responses to

standalone requests. When a CR 34 request is coupled with a deposition notice under

CR 30.02(5), the production or inspection takes place at the deposition.

       This reading is consistent with the structure of both rules. CR 34.02 requires

a party to "serve a written response within 30 days"—meaning the responding party

has 30 days to state its objections in writing. Nothing in CR 34.02(2) requires that

the actual inspection be deferred until 30 days after service of the request; it requires

written objections within 30 days. UofL is free to have served those written

objections—and it did, by filing this motion—but the filing of a protective order

motion does not establish that the inspection itself is improper.

       In any event, the notice expressly offered to adjust the June 4 date. Had

UofL's counsel promptly contacted Plaintiffs' counsel upon receiving the Notice—

rather than waiting six days to file a motion for protective order—the parties could

have selected a mutually agreeable date well within any 30-day period.

       B.     UofL's incomplete production under court order makes the live
              inspection necessary.

       Even if there were some procedural question about the timing of an inspection

request, it is rendered academic by UofL's deficient production. UofL was ordered to

"fully and completely respond" to Plaintiffs' 4/13/2026 discovery by May 29, 2026.

That discovery specifically requested:

       •      the complete EMR audit trails for Dr. Gettis from July 2022 through
              May 2024 (Request No. 1);
       •      the complete MyChart patient portal audit trail log for Timmi's account
              (Request No. 2);
       •      all documents showing the state and contents of Dr. Gettis's In-Basket
              from August 15, 2022 through April 24, 2024 (Request No. 3);
       •      screenshots or system-generated images of Dr. Gettis's inbox on the
              date the PET scan result was received and when it was acknowledged
              (Request No. 4); and
       •      all technical documentation describing how Epic's acknowledgment
              function works (Request No. 5).

       None of that was produced. Instead, UofL produced seven documents, most of

which are generic Epic workflow tip sheets publicly available to any Epic user. The

audit data that has been produced contains suspicious access patterns—including

what appears to be numerous entries accessing Timmi's MyChart records under the

designation "MyChart" around critical time periods—but the produced data omits IP

addresses, patient notification records, record-specific logging, URLs, print detail

fields, and In-Basket audit data. These are not marginal or exotic data fields. They

are the fields that would reveal whether anyone actually read Timmi's PET scan

result, whether the patient was ever notified of the result, and what exactly happened

in that system between August 2022 and the first time anyone told Timmi her scan

showed something alarming.

       Plaintiffs do not seek unrestricted access to UofL's systems out of curiosity.

Plaintiffs seek to understand the audit data UofL has produced—data that raises

more questions than it answers—and to fill the gaps in what UofL failed to produce

despite a court order directing full compliance. That is a far cry from the "mere

suspicion" and "speculation" that courts have found insufficient to justify inspection.

Cf. Scotts Co. LLC v. Liberty Mut. Ins. Co., 2007 WL 1723509, at *2 (S.D. Ohio June

12, 2007).

IV.    THE LIVE INSPECTION IS APPROPRIATE, LIMITED IN SCOPE, AND
       CAN BE CONDUCTED WITH ADEQUATE SAFEGUARDS.

       A.     The functioning of UofL's EHR system—not just the data it
              stored—is directly at issue in this case.

       The Sedona Principles, which UofL itself cites, acknowledge that live

inspection is appropriate where "there is a substantial need to discover information

about the computer system and programs used (as opposed to the data stored on that

system)." The Sedona Principles, Third Edition, 19 Sedona Conf. J. 1, at Comment

6.d. (2018). This is precisely such a case.

       What does it mean in Epic's In-Basket system for a result to be

"acknowledged"? Can a result be "acknowledged" without the provider ever opening

it? Does the acknowledgment function in Epic automatically trigger the release of

results to MyChart, regardless of whether the provider has reviewed them? Do

entries in the audit log distinguish between a provider who opened a result and read

it versus one who bulk-acknowledged a queue? These are questions about how the

system itself functions—not just what data it stored. The only way to answer them is

to see the live system and walk through the workflow with a designee who knows it.

       UofL's argument that "those matters may be explored through targeted written

discovery requests" rings hollow. Plaintiffs served exactly that targeted written

discovery on April 13, 2026. UofL failed to respond to it for over a month. The Court

had to order compliance. Even then, UofL's production was incomplete. Written

discovery has proven inadequate. A live demonstration of the system—in which a

knowledgeable designee navigates Epic's In-Basket, audit trail, and MyChart

functions while being questioned about Timmi's specific records—is the most direct

and efficient path to the truth.

       B.     The inspection is limited to one patient's records.

       UofL warns of catastrophic HIPAA violations and exposure of "dozens of

uninterested individuals." This is not the inspection Plaintiffs seek. The Notice

requests "live access to all EHRs relating to Thelma ('Timmi') Tinker." Plaintiffs are

not asking to search the records of any other patient. The designee controls the

navigation. If, in demonstrating how the system works, adjacent patient information

would appear on screen, the designee can scroll past it—just as UofL's own staff do

hundreds of times daily without triggering a HIPAA catastrophe. UofL's suggestion

that demonstrating one patient's record on Zoom screen-share will "inevitably"

expose protected information about dozens of patients is unsupported speculation.

       C.     Plaintiffs are open to a reasonable inspection protocol.

       The Sedona Principles identify certain best practices for EHR inspections,

including a documented agreed-upon protocol that protects third-party information

and is narrowly tailored to litigation issues. Plaintiffs are amenable to negotiating

and agreeing to such a protocol. Plaintiffs are not seeking to roam freely through

UofL's server infrastructure. They seek to understand one deceased patient's EHR,

the audit trail associated with it, and how the specific functions of Epic's system

operated with respect to her PET scan result. A reasonable protocol—limiting

navigation to Timmi's records, having screenshots captured contemporaneously as

exhibits, conducted via Zoom so the screen can be monitored—accomplishes the

inspection without the HIPAA apocalypse UofL conjures.

       D.     UofL's cited cases are distinguishable.

       John B. v. Goetz, 531 F.3d 448 (6th Cir. 2008), involved sweeping "mirror

imaging" of computer systems where the connection between the devices and the

claims was "unduly vague or unsubstantiated." That is not this case. Plaintiffs are

not asking to image UofL's servers. They are asking UofL's own designee to navigate

one patient's existing EHR record and explain what the audit data shows. The

connection between Timmi's Epic record and the claims in this lawsuit is about as

direct as any such connection could be.

       Scotts Co. LLC v. Liberty Mut. Ins. Co., 2007 WL 1723509 (S.D. Ohio June

12, 2007), involved a plaintiff who could offer only suspicion that documents were

being withheld. Plaintiffs here have actual evidence: a court order that UofL failed

to honor, a last-minute production that is incomplete, an audit log with missing

fields, and suspicious access patterns that raise specific, articulable questions about

what happened to Timmi's scan result. That is good cause—not mere suspicion.

V.     COMBINING THE CR 30.02(6) DEPOSITION WITH THE INSPECTION
       IS MORE EFFICIENT, NOT LESS.

       UofL argues that combining the deposition with the inspection is procedurally

improper and "conflates two distinct discovery mechanisms." But CR 30.02(5)

expressly contemplates exactly this combination. And there is an obvious practical

reason why: a designee who can explain how the system works while demonstrating

it is more informative than separate witnesses testifying in the abstract.

       UofL's witnesses are the persons best positioned to navigate the system. Their

testimony will be most meaningful if they can show as well as tell. A bifurcated

approach—testimony first, inspection later with different personnel—is more

burdensome, not less, and creates the risk of inconsistency between what the

corporate designee says and what the system shows.

       If the Court has concerns about combining the two mechanisms, Plaintiffs

respectfully request at a minimum that the corporate deposition proceed on all

eighteen topics—including live demonstration of MyChart, PACS, In-Basket, and

audit trail navigation—with any standalone CR 34 inspection to follow promptly

thereafter.

VI.    UofL'S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE NOTICE'S TIMING IS
       MISLEADING.

       UofL opens its motion with the observation that the Notice was served "on

Friday evening of Memorial Day weekend" to suggest that Plaintiffs were acting in

bad faith. But the Notice expressly stated that Plaintiffs were open to adjusting the

date. What UofL omits is that UofL created this compressed timeline. UofL ignored

Plaintiffs' discovery from April 13 through May 13. UofL then promised to produce

"by end of week" on May 18, and failed to do so. The Court had to order compliance.

UofL produced an inadequate production at 3:50 p.m. on the last possible day.

       Plaintiffs have expert disclosures due June 3, 2026 and a trial date of

November 30, 2026. They were not creating "tactical pressure." They were attempting

to salvage a discovery schedule that UofL's own dilatory conduct had compressed.

       The appropriate response to that context is not to reward UofL's delay by

foreclosing Plaintiffs' ability to conduct the discovery they need. It is to ensure the

discovery goes forward promptly, as the court-ordered discovery schedule requires.
